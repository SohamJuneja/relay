Bitcoin spent the hour going nowhere, and that is the story

Four fifteen-minute windows, four closes inside a forty-dollar band. The tape is not quiet because nobody is trading. It is quiet because everybody agrees.

By Marguerite Okonjo, Markets — 5 min read

The most interesting hour of trading I have watched this month produced almost no price movement at all. Bitcoin opened the first fifteen-minute window a shade under seventy-nine thousand three hundred, closed it eleven dollars higher, and then did approximately that three more times. If you had looked only at the hourly candle you would have concluded nothing happened. The order book says otherwise.

What actually happened is that the short-dated event contracts on this venue spent the hour pricing the next quarter of an hour at something very close to a coin flip, and the market makers were content to let them. The up side traded between forty-six and fifty-four cents on the dollar for most of the hour. That is not indecision. That is a market that has correctly identified that it does not know, and has priced accordingly, which is a rarer and more disciplined thing than it sounds.

The quiet is expensive

Every one of these windows is a self-contained contract. It opens, it takes orders for fifteen minutes, the oracle reads a price, and it settles. There is no position to carry, no funding to pay, no liquidation to fear. The entire risk of the instrument is the fifteen minutes in front of you. That structure is supposed to make it cheap to have an opinion, and for the taker it does.

For the maker it does the opposite. A market maker quoting a fifteen-minute binary at fifty cents is writing an option that expires before they can hedge it in any meaningful venue. The spread has to pay for that, and on the thinner series it plainly does not — which is why, on a venue processing more than eleven thousand fills a day, roughly a third of completed windows close with no trade in them at all. Not because nobody quoted. Because somebody quoted on both sides and nobody took it.

A third of the windows on this venue are offered and refused. That is not a liquidity problem. It is a distribution problem wearing a liquidity problem's clothes.

I keep coming back to that number because it reframes what the venue's problem actually is. The books are there. The settlement works. The oracle answers in about a second. What is missing is anybody arriving with an opinion at the moment the window is open, and the reason is that the window is open for fifteen minutes and the people with opinions are somewhere else — reading, arguing, scrolling — during all of them.

Where the order comes from

Which is roughly why you are looking at a trading card in the margin of a newspaper column. The card is not ours; it belongs to the venue, and it arrived here as two lines of markup. What it does carry is a code identifying this publication, written into the order itself, so that when one of you takes a position the venue can see where the order came from and pay whoever sent it. That mechanism is not new — every exchange has some version of it — but the thing being paid for here is unusually honest. It is not a referral link or a tracking pixel. It is a field in the transaction.

I am ambivalent about the model and comfortable saying so in the same column that hosts it. Publications embedding a live market next to commentary about that market is a conflict that deserves naming, which is why this piece is filed as opinion and why the card says what it costs before you touch it. But the alternative on offer — that trading venues remain places you must already have decided to visit — has produced a venue where a third of the windows go untraded. That is a worse outcome for everyone, including the readers who never hear about it.

What to watch next

The five-minute series is the one to watch. It carries the most fills and the widest spreads, which usually means the makers there are being paid properly for the risk and the takers are paying for immediacy. If those spreads compress while the fill count holds, somebody has found a hedge. If the fill count collapses instead, the makers have simply left. Either would tell us more about this market's structure than another hour of forty-dollar candles.
